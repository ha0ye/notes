Hi, my name is Hao Ye. For the last 3 years, I have been the Reproducibility Librarian at the University of Florida, where I have been responsible for training for and institution-wide implementation of open science.

I have several comments about how to more broadly shift the US research ecosystem towards open science practices.

First, policy alone is insufficient, and practical guidance is sorely needed. With the rollout of the 2023 NIH Data Management and Sharing Policy, many were left confused about how to write a plan or choose a repository. In leaving the policy vague to accommodate diverse needs, there was no clear pathway for researchers to comply with the policy. And it is unreasonable to expect researchers, research support staff, or administrators to read the numerous publications on "best practices" and then to devise what to implement in each case. To deal with this problem, I led a group of data librarians from around the country to create guidance materials, but it would have been much more effective to be in a working partnership with policymakers at NIH. From my understanding there is an internal rubric used to evaluate the new DMS plans. Ironically, this rubric to evaluate data sharing plans is itself NOT openly shared...

Second, I'm sure other commenters have remarked on the importance of incentives. While I do not disagree, I would like to emphasize the dangers of relying on easily quantifiable metrics over the need to support research that does not fit into neat "boxes" of preregistration and hypothesis-testing frameworks. 

Third, many researchers, especially early career researchers, are already motivated to adopt open practices. However, it can be demotivating when they see few of their peers doing so, when senior researchers are understandably wary of the additional time and effort required, and when various instances of "closed" practices are not corrected. Making open science commonplace requires upholding community norms about open, effective, and rigorous research. We already have plenty of tools and technologies for open science; infrastructure investments should also include people and communities to supporting these social aspects to shift practices.
