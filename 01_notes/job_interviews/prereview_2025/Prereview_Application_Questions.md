#### Social Media & Community Engagement: Tell us about a time you used social media or community management tools to increase engagement. What strategies worked well? What didn’t? What did you learn?

While a reproducibility librarian at the University of Florida, I was a member of the social media team for the Health Science Center Libraries. My responsibilities included all aspects of creating social media posts: following our internal style guide, writing copy, selecting or designing graphics (from our photo library, or public domain / free stock image sources), and creating alt text.

The typical goal of a post would be to promote a new event or resource relevant to our audience of students and researchers across the health science colleges at the university. What I learned was that conversion is really really hard! Perhaps unsurprisingly, the most popular posts involved announcements of therapy dog visits to campus around exam time.

A key lesson for me was that a typical use of social media might be to seek out entertainment rather than in-depth news or educational content. This then suggests that an intermediate goal could be to design something entertaining to convert users, and to then promote this entertaining event on social media.

Conversely, when trusted champions or ambassadors promoted events within their local communities, the message was more successful than broadcast messages. This suggests identifying such individuals and tailoring content for them to amplify.

#### Communicating Complex Ideas: How do you approach translating complex or technical ideas into accessible, compelling narratives for diverse audiences? Walk us through your process.

My approach to communicating a complex idea has two steps:
(1) Identify a singular message or takeaway.
(2) Draw a clear connection from the content of (1) to a problem or value that is relevant for the audience.
These steps don't necessarily occur in order or only once! Often, I might go back and forth between (1) and (2).

In my current work of creating rigor training, I am often communicating a complex rigor topic to audiences spanning academic disciplines and/or experience in research. Unlike a classroom setting where everyone is at the same stage, I have to find common ground for both the audience and the content of the message.

As an example, in our current unit on the principles of causality, one lesson is about identifying how an individual research project connects to other evidence in supporting a scientific theory. I would start by using short examples to establish a few points:
a) improperly identifying a non-causal study as causal evidence can lead to dead ends or spurious conclusions
b) impactful research accurately communicates how an individual study sheds light on an area of ambiguity in a theory

These points could then be connected to the takeaway that proper contextualization of research saves time and produces better work. This goal can resonate with our audience, and provide the motivation to learn the skills for proper (causal) contextualization. Note that the message or takeaway does not have to encompass the whole topic, but serve the purpose of engaging someone to take the desired action of investing effort in the learning.

#### Equity, Diversity, Inclusion, and Accessibility: What do equity, diversity, inclusion, and accessibility mean to you? Share an example of how you’ve applied these principles in your work or community involvement.

Equity, diversity, inclusion, and accessibility (EDIA) all have somewhat different individual meanings, but broadly speaking, I interpret EDIA as pointing in the direction of valuing people as individuals and welcoming their input and participation in communities and workplaces.

I believe that a key requirement to upholding EDIA principles on a day-to-day basis involves personal humility and openness about how to complete a task or objective. This does not mean that every single decision is open for debate, but I think it does mean that the modes of communication and decision-making within a workplace be intentional in encouraging different perspectives and ways of interacting (provided that they do not conflict with core values).

There are multiple levels at which I have fostered a culture that supports EDIA. For example, in my current workplace, I have designed and implemented a "How to Work with Me" document as a way to encourage intentional reflection on communication and collaboration practices. In other settings, I have also received and acted on related training:

Since receiving training in Ally Skills from Dr. Sheila Addison (formerly of Frame Shift Consulting), I have taught Ally Skills workshops four times; most recently with Esther Plomp for OLS (formerly Open Life Science) a few months ago.

As a member of the Code of Conduct Committee for Reproducibility for Everyone, I helped co-create the code of conduct, and have responsibilities in handling incident reports.

#### Understanding Openness: What does “open” mean to you in the context of scholarly communication? Feel free to include values, practices, or challenges that shape your perspective.

As a former computational ecologist, "open" to me means that all steps and intermediate products that occur in the process of conducting research are shared (to the extent that it is reasonable to do so).

I distinctly remember a moment towards the end of my PhD, when I tried to reproduce the results of a published paper. After exchanging some emails with the authors, they still would not share their code, but only linked the software library that they used. I spent several frustrating months trying to reproduce their analysis without getting anywhere. Some years later I talked with a colleague who also unsuccessfully tried to reproduce their results! Although it validated my experience, I still do not know to what extent the results of that paper might have been problematic or depended on key methodological details that were not shared.

I don't think my own experience is unique; many of the choices that occur during the design, execution, and reporting of research projects are not thoroughly and transparently reported. The extent to which this impacts science is not well-known, but it seems likely that there are some negative impacts in the rigor of research results (e.g. irreproducibility due to unreported errors/issues or general vagueness), and in the equity of credit (e.g. acknowledging contributors and sources). 

Since my PhD and participation in the Mozilla Open Leaders program, I have steered my work to address both of the axes above---improving rigor and reproducibility through education, and encouraging credit through changing systems of sharing and rewards.

#### Fostering Belonging: Can you share an example of when you helped foster (or witnessed the creation of) a culture of belonging in a community or workplace? What was your role?

In 2022 I joined a team of four data paper editors for the journal, Ecology, and in 2023 I was the lead organizer of a special session, "Designing the future of open data: A discussion with the Data Paper editors of 'Ecology'", at the annual meeting of the Ecological Society of America.

Our goal for the session was to gather information from our audience of ecological researchers on their perspectives on publishing ecological data in the journal. Some of our specific interests were to identify changes that could:
* improve submission and review processes
* increase submissions, and their diversity and quality
* align practices with open science movements (e.g. data sharing broadly, and other forms of open science)

In designing the session as an unconference-style guided discussion, we had concerns about how best to engage a group of strangers, of mixed career stages, about a topic that could have many different personal opinions. In the end, we settled on several design aspects:
1. organizing the participants into small groups so that individuals would have more opportunity to share
2. using an introduction activity to normalize both the sharing of a personal perspective, and the practice of sticking post-it notes to a public board to give input

The introduction prompt we used was to write "one emoji you would use to describe your subfield, study organism, or study biome (OR if none exists, what emoji would you add to the existing standard)". This had the further effect of normalizing the subtext that there were no wrong answers when sharing a personal perspective!
