Ok, here we go...

A male employee receiving low scores from a predominantly female work group for being "aggressive" and "too self-confident" is NOT evidence for bias. Yes, performance evaluations are subjective, yes a majority female workplace may have implicit social norms that are being violated, yes, not receiving constructive feedback is bad workplace policy. None of these are actually evidence of bias UNLESS he is being treated differently or unfairly compared to female colleagues. We don't know that he is! The "correct answer" that he is ASSUMES that the female colleagues will be biased to rate Tom as "aggressive" and "too self-confident"!!! ARGH

"Research shows that giving positive feedback to people reduces the likelihood that they will act in a biased way in the workplace." Ahhh, the call to unity

"What helps draw the line? An organization's ability to incorporate a robust and thoughtful diversity program." which is ... ???

"Establishing structure in policies and practices reduces ambiguous standards that allow bias to roam. Structure should be informed by data and other concrete measures that add objectivity, an integral part of any foundation." AHHHH THE DATA IS BIASED!!

Really, what research on diversity teams and diversity staff positions?

"Is Greg's decision that Nora is not social a bias?" It's not the decision that she's not social that is the issue, it's the decision to not include her in the team happy hour. Those are different things! You can have opinions that your coworkers are not social! You can't decide who to invite to workplace gatherings on that basis! STOP CONFLATING THE TWO!

I have training and experience in leading workshops on inclusion and equity, so the topic of managing bias is not new to me. While I did not expect this training to include a substantial amount of new information to me, I did expect it to not be harmful, and it fails on that (more details below). In addition, it is curious that the introduction focuses on racism and racial bias, yet none of the examples cover this axis. Two scenarios discuss gender, one example is about requiring photos with job applications, and another example is about eliminating a perk that disproportionately affects employees who live in a lower-income area.

The presentation of the first case study is flawed. It contains a situation where a male employee receives low scores on a workplace evaluation from his work group in a predominantly female work environment - he is told that he can sometimes be "aggressive" and too self-confident. We are also told that he receives high marks for performance from his supervisors. We are supposed to infer that the comments from colleagues are the result of bias against his gender; however it is possible that he is alienating his colleagues by being aggressive and arrogant in his interactions, and that these negative interactions would not appear in the work products evaluated by his supervisors. In fact, there is substantial evidence that men interrupt more and take up more speaking time in group discussions, and fail to volunteer for unvalued labor, such as taking minutes. While it *is* true that the lack of a rigorous mechanism for colleague evaluations *could* lead to bias, the suggested answer that there is not only bias on the basis of gender, and along stereotypical gender lines is harmful. I have strong worries that many who take this training will simply conclude that anytime a male employee is being told by female colleagues that he is being aggressive and too self-confident that these comments are the result of an anti-male gender bias.

Second, the training is inadequate in explaining the difference between personal judgments, and decisions based on those judgments that cause inequity. For example, in the case study where a coworker who did not participate in golfing excursions was excluded from team happy hours, the suggested solution was to find other activities that the coworker could participate in. In reality, the issue is not that a group of colleagues golf together outside of work, nor that the coworker is perceived to be less social, but that a *workplace* decision re: team happy hour was based on that personal judgment. Employees should not be expected to find ways to socialize outside of work (though they certainly can, and this shouldn't be frowned upon.) Further, happy hours are frequently exclusionary due to location, timing, and the presence of alcohol - a note should be added on team-building activities that are more inclusive!

Third, one of the recommendations is: "Establish[ing] structure in policies and practices reduces ambiguous standards that allow bias to roam. Structure should be informed by data and other concrete measures that add objectivity, an integral part of any foundation." While I agree that ambiguous standards are detrimental, the concept that data and metrics add objectivity is false. In the area of ethical AI and machine learning, there are numerous issues with both the data, algorithms, and models that can either recapitulate biases and discrimination or amplify them.

Fourth, in the description of short term thinking, the word "myopia" is used. Although one of the definitions for myopia *is* a lack of foresight in decision-making, it is also a visual impairment, and therefore this usage in a negative connotation is ableist.

Please actually have these materials reviewed by a DEI expert before requiring them of everyone!
